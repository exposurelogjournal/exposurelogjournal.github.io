Point a camera upward at a tall building and its walls seem to tip inward at the top. Photographers call this converging verticals, or keystoning. The cause is the angle of the camera, so you can prevent most of it before you press the shutter. What remains can be corrected in editing, within limits.

## What makes vertical lines converge?

Vertical lines converge when the camera's sensor is not parallel to the wall you are photographing. Tilt the camera up and the top of the building sits farther from the sensor than its base, so the top is drawn smaller and the edges lean together.

The usual case is a tall building shot from street level with the camera pointing upward. That situation is described under [perspective control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_control): the building appears to fall away from you. The lens is working normally; you are seeing ordinary perspective applied to a subject that is much taller than you are.

A wide lens makes the effect more obvious. To fit a tower into a 16 mm frame you stand close and tilt steeply, so the lean is strong. You can see the same geometry on any [3D model platform](https://sketchfab.com/anjajepsen): tilt the virtual camera upward at a block-shaped building and its vertical edges start to converge at once.

Horizontal lines behave the same way when you shoot a façade at an angle. Vertical lines get the attention because the eye expects walls to stand straight.

## How do you avoid converging lines on location?

Keep the camera level, move back, and frame wider than you need. If the building has to fill the frame and you cannot step back, a shift lens lets you keep the sensor vertical and still include the top.

Work through the shoot in this order:

1. Scout the viewpoint. A higher position, such as a first-floor window or a footbridge opposite, reduces the tilt you need.
2. Step back and lengthen the focal length. Moving from 24 mm to 70 mm, with enough distance to match, cuts the upward angle needed to include the roofline.
3. Level the camera. Switch on the electronic level or the grid in live view and line a wall edge up with a grid line.
4. Frame loose. Leave extra foreground and sky so you have room to crop later.
5. Fix the exposure. On a tripod, ISO 100, f/8 to f/11 and a 2-second timer give a sharp frame from edge to edge.

Interiors and public buildings often come with photography rules, so check opening hours and permissions in a [city culture directory](https://www.deutsche-staedte.de/muenchen/kultur/eintraege.php?start=80331Muenchen) before you travel.

A shift lens moves the lens up relative to the sensor while the camera stays level. Many tilt-shift lenses offer around 10 to 12 mm of shift, but check the specification of yours. Set the camera level, shift upward until the top of the building enters the frame, then shoot. View cameras do the same with a movement called rise, which raises the lens parallel to the film. Shift lenses are typically manual focus and cost more than a standard lens of the same focal length.

| Method | What it does | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| Step back, use a longer lens | Reduces the tilt needed | Needs space and a clear view |
| Raise the camera position | Reduces the tilt needed | Needs access to a higher spot |
| Shift lens | Keeps verticals parallel at capture | Cost, manual focus, one focal length |
| Shoot level, crop later | Keeps verticals parallel | Wastes part of the frame |

## How do you correct leaning lines in editing?

Correct lens distortion first, then apply a vertical perspective correction, then crop. Most editors do this with one slider or an automatic mode, and you finish by checking that the building still looks natural.

1. Apply the lens profile. This removes barrel distortion, which bends lines before any perspective correction can straighten them.
2. Correct the verticals. In the Transform panel of Lightroom Classic, the Vertical mode straightens converging lines in one step. Use the manual sliders when the automatic result tips the whole frame.
3. Restore the proportions. Correction stretches the top of the image sideways and the façade can look squat. Stretch it vertically by a few per cent until windows look square again.
4. Crop. The correction leaves empty wedges at the corners, so crop them away or let the editor constrain the crop for you.
5. Export for the destination. A 2048 px long edge in sRGB works for a [simple one-page site](https://mcnauengs-yeugh-loms.yolasite.com/), and the full-resolution file stays untouched for print.

## What can editing not fix?

Software cannot add detail that was never captured. A strong correction costs resolution at the top of the frame, can cut away a large share of the picture, and cannot repair the stretching a wide lens puts into the corners.

A steep upward tilt needs a large correction. The top of the image is enlarged more than the bottom, so fine detail such as brickwork looks softer there than at street level. Cropping away the wedges shrinks the frame, and filling them with generated content tends to fail on repeating patterns like window grids.

Wide lenses also stretch objects near the edges of the frame, so a person or a round column in a corner looks wider than it is. Perspective correction does not undo this. A longer lens avoids it, and so does a shift lens, because the camera does not need to tilt.

Full correction can also look wrong. A tall building with perfectly parallel sides, shot from the pavement, can look top heavy. Leave a small trace of convergence in place when the result feels off.

## When are converging lines the better choice?

Converging lines work when they are clearly deliberate. A steep upward angle on a tall façade exaggerates its height and leads the eye to the top, while a slight accidental lean only looks like a mistake.

To use the effect, stand close, use a 16 to 24 mm lens and tilt up firmly. Frame the building symmetrically so the edges meet near the centre of the frame, or let a staircase or a glass roof draw the lines towards a single point, which makes the lean part of the composition.

Keep the angle consistent across a set. When several frames share the same steep viewpoint, the lines read as a choice, particularly in a series on [a small web page](http://anjajepsen.onlc.fr/).

## Which approach should you use?

Decide at the scouting stage. If you can step back or get higher, do that and keep the camera level. Where neither is possible and you shoot buildings often, a shift lens pays for itself in saved editing and cleaner files.

With a standard lens, shoot level and wide, crop, and correct lightly in editing. When the scene calls for drama, skip correction entirely, tilt the camera up hard and build the frame around the converging lines.
