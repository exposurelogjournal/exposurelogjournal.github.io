A photographic print depends on its light-sensitive coating and on how it was exposed and washed. Between the 1840s and today, four main processes shaped those choices. Knowing which one made a print tells you how to handle it and how long it should last.

## What were the first photographic prints made of?

The first widely used paper print was the albumen print, published in 1847. It coats paper with egg white (albumen) and salt, then sensitises it with silver nitrate once the coating has dried.

Albumen paper was a printing-out process: the image appears under light alone, with no developer. You laid the negative on the paper in a frame, so the two touched (a contact print, the same size as the negative), and exposed it to daylight. Direct sunlight was the traditional source. Gold or selenium toning, a bath that deposits a more stable metal on the image, improved the tone and protected the image against fading.

Albumen became the dominant form of photographic positive from 1855 to the start of the twentieth century, with a peak between 1860 and 1890. The carte de visite, a small portrait mounted on card, spread on albumen paper.

## How did silver gelatin and the darkroom change printing?

Gelatin silver paper replaced printing-out with developing-out. A short exposure creates an invisible latent image, and a chemical developer makes it visible. Printing no longer depended on strong daylight, and an enlarger could project a small negative onto a much bigger sheet.

Commercial gelatin silver paper existed by 1874, but its quality was poor, and the paper became widespread in the 1890s. A sheet has four layers: a paper base, a white baryta coating that gives a smooth surface, a gelatin layer holding tiny silver particles, and a thin protective overcoat.

A traditional black-and-white session follows the same sequence every time:

1. Switch on a safelight, a dim amber or red lamp that black-and-white paper does not react to.
2. Place the paper under the enlarger, which is a vertical projector that casts the negative's image onto the sheet.
3. Expose a test strip in steps of a few seconds to find the right exposure, then expose the full sheet.
4. Slide the sheet into the developer tray at about 20 °C for the time on the paper maker's data sheet.
5. Move it through a stop bath, a mild acid that halts development, and then into the fixer.
6. Wash the print and dry it.

## How did colour prints arrive?

Through the chromogenic print, also called a C-print or Type-C print. It has three gelatin layers, each sensitive to one colour of light. During colour development, the blue-sensitive layer forms yellow dye, the green-sensitive layer forms magenta dye, and the red-sensitive layer forms cyan dye.

The first commercial chromogenic print was Agfacolor Neu in 1936. Kodak followed with Kodacolor in 1942, a simpler and cheaper alternative, and released Type C paper for professional use in 1955, the paper that gave the C-print its name.

Today's chromogenic prints use the RA-4 process, and papers such as Kodak Endura and Fujifilm Crystal Archive are common. A lab can expose the paper from a digital file with a laser or LED, so a single machine prints everything from holiday landscapes to [Christmas photos taken at home](https://mama-hilft.de/weihnachtsshooting-familie/). Digital chromogenic prints are still in wide use for printing photographs.

## What is a giclée print?

Giclée describes digital fine-art prints made on inkjet printers. The word comes from the French gicleur, meaning nozzle, and was coined around 1990 for fine-art prints made on modified Iris printers, to set them apart from the commercial proofs those machines were built for.

Today the term covers high-quality inkjet printing with archival inks. Printers typically use CMYK inks (cyan, magenta, yellow and black) plus extra cartridges for a wider colour range. The paper can be photo paper, watercolour paper, cotton canvas or textured vinyl. The word on its own says little about quality, so ask which ink and which paper a lab uses. Because an inkjet needs only a file, the same image can reach a gallery wall, an album or [an event platform](https://anjajepsen.kktix.cc/) without a negative or an enlarger.

## How long does each type of print last?

It depends on the image material and on handling. A silver print that was fixed and washed properly can last a very long time, a dye print fades as its dyes break down, and a pigment-ink print resists fading better than a dye-ink one.

With gelatin silver prints, the order of steps matters: after development, [fixing with sodium thiosulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelatin_silver_process) removes the remaining light-sensitive silver halides, and a water wash follows. Toning adds stability by coating the silver with a less easily oxidised metal such as gold, or by converting it to silver selenide or silver sulfide. Catalogues that record a print's process, date and support as [structured data](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q138863770) are useful for this, because the process tells you how the print will age and how to store it.

| Process | Image is made of | What helps it last |
|---|---|---|
| Albumen print | Silver in an egg-white layer | Gold or selenium toning |
| Gelatin silver print | Silver particles in gelatin | Thorough fixing and washing, optional toning |
| Chromogenic print | Three layers of dye | Storage away from light and heat |
| Giclée inkjet print | Pigment or dye ink on paper | Pigment inks, archival paper, UV-filtering glazing |

Even a pigment-ink print depends on archival paper, UV-filtering glass and sensible framing conditions to look on the wall as it did on day one.

## How do you choose a print process today?

For a colour image from a digital file, order a lab chromogenic print or a pigment-ink inkjet print. For black and white with the look of a traditional darkroom, a gelatin silver print is the match, though you need a darkroom or a lab that still offers it.

Check the lab before you order. Read what other customers say, for instance on [a website review profile](https://allare.io/businesses/anjajepsen-de), and ask which paper and ink it uses. Then order a 10 × 15 cm test print on two papers, one glossy and one matt. Gloss shows fine detail more clearly, and matt cuts reflections behind glass.

Hold both prints under the light where the final picture will hang. The one that shows your shadows cleanly in that light is the one to scale up.
