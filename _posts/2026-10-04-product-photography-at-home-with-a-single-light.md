One light is enough for clean product photos if you control where it sits, what it bounces off and how the camera reads its colour. For most small items, the kit is a window or a single lamp, a sheet of white card and a tripod.

## Which single light should you use?

For small items, a window gives soft light for free. A daylight-balanced lamp is the better pick when you need the same result at any hour. Either works if you switch off every other light in the room.

Next to a small product, a window is a large source, and that size is what makes the light soft. The drawback is that it moves. Cloud and time of day change both brightness and colour, and so does the season. By the time [the light at sunset](http://www.articles.howto-tips.com/How-To-do-things-in-2020/babybauch-shootings-bei-sonnenuntergang) reaches the glass, it has turned warm, so a frame from noon and one from the evening will not match. If you have a batch to shoot, finish it within the same hour.

A lamp gives you consistency. Look for an LED panel or bulb marked daylight, roughly 5,000 to 6,500 K. K stands for kelvin, the scale for colour temperature: lower numbers look orange, higher numbers look blue. A bare bulb is a small source and makes hard shadows, so put diffusion in front of it. Baking parchment, a thin white sheet or a translucent shower curtain all work. The larger the diffuser and the closer it sits to the product, the softer the light.

## How do you arrange the set?

Put the product on a table beside the light, with the light coming from the side. Place a white card on the far side, set the camera on a tripod and fire it with a timer.

1. Tape a sheet of white paper to a wall and curve it down onto the table without a crease. This curve is called a sweep, and it removes the horizon line behind the product.
2. Position the table so the light arrives from the side, at about 90 degrees to the line between camera and product. With a window, that means the table sits beside the glass, not in front of it.
3. Place the product on the paper. The gap between the product and the wall decides how bright the background looks: close gives a lighter background, further away lets it fall darker.
4. Stand a reflector opposite the light, just outside the frame.
5. Mount the camera on the tripod at product height, or slightly above for flat items such as jewellery.
6. Trigger with the two-second self-timer so your hand never touches the camera.

## How does a reflector shape the shadows?

A white card on the shadow side lifts the shadows. The closer it sits, the brighter the fill. A black card in the same place does the opposite and deepens them.

White foam board is the usual choice because it fills evenly. Smooth kitchen foil taped to card gives a stronger, more directional fill. Distance matters more than most beginners expect. Light intensity from a point source is inversely proportional to [the square of the distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law), so moving a lamp from 50 cm to 100 cm leaves a quarter of the light on the product. Small moves near the lamp change the exposure a lot. When the shadows need adjusting, move the card and leave the lamp alone.

## Which camera settings suit product shots?

Use a low ISO, a mid-range aperture and a tripod. The tripod lets the shutter stay open as long as the exposure needs, so you never have to raise ISO to avoid blur.

| Setting | Starting value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mode | Manual or aperture priority | Keeps exposure steady between frames |
| ISO | 100 or your camera's base value | Keeps noise low |
| Aperture | f/8 to f/11 | Gives enough depth of field, the zone that looks sharp, for a mug or bottle |
| Shutter speed | From 1/15 s to a few seconds | The tripod absorbs the long time |
| Focal length | 50 to 100 mm (full-frame equivalent) | Less distortion than a wide lens |
| Exposure compensation | +1 to +1.5 EV on white paper | The meter renders white as grey |

Check the histogram after the first frame. For a white background, the right-hand edge should sit near the edge of the graph without touching it.

## How do you set white balance?

Photograph a grey card in the same light, then use that frame to set a custom white balance. If you shoot RAW, you can also pick the grey in editing. Avoid auto white balance, which shifts from frame to frame.

Mixed light is a common cause of colour casts. Daylight from a window combined with a warm household bulb gives two different colours on one product, and no single setting fixes both. Turn the room lights off and close any door that lets in a different colour. If you have to type a value, start near the figure printed on the lamp packaging, often about 5,500 K for a daylight lamp, and adjust until the paper looks neutral.

## How do you deal with reflections and hard shadows?

Reflections come from the size and position of the light. Make the source larger with diffusion, or move it until its reflection slides off the product and away from the lens. Hard shadows mean the source is too small, so add diffusion or bring the white card closer.

Glossy surfaces mirror whatever bright things sit in front of them, such as the window, the lamp or your own white shirt. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, so walk the light around the product in small steps and watch the glare move. Wear dark clothing and hang a black card behind the camera to stop your own reflection appearing.

Glass and polished metal need a larger, softer source. A big diffused panel beside the object gives a smooth gradient along its edges instead of a hot spot. A soft contact shadow under the product is fine, because it anchors the item to the surface. Zoom in to 100 per cent before you pack up the set, since dust and fingerprints show up there first.

## How do you prepare and share the finished photos?

Correct white balance on the whole batch at once, crop every image to the same proportions and export as sRGB JPEG at the size your platform asks for. Keep the first frame of each session as a reference for brightness and colour.

Product images also belong wherever your business has a profile, for example on [a startup platform](https://www.fundable.com/user-820159), next to your own shop pages. For a social profile that only allows one web address, [a simple link page](https://666742.8b.io/) can point to the full set.

## When is a home setup not enough?

A single light handles small, matte or lightly glossy items well. Large furniture, mirror-finish metal, or a catalogue of hundreds of products with identical lighting call for more lights and more room.

At that point, [a photo studio directory](https://www.fotostudio.net/münchen/anja-jepsen-fotografie-11108405.html) is a quick way to compare studios that take on larger jobs. Until then, start with one object, one window, a white card and a tripod. Shoot it, change a single variable, such as the card distance, and shoot it again. Compare the two frames at full size and keep whichever setting gave the cleaner shadow.
