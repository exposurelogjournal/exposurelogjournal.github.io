Choose a 35 mm lens if you mostly photograph people in their surroundings: streets, rooms, markets, travel scenes. Choose a 50 mm lens if you want tighter framing, simple head-and-shoulders portraits and a calmer background. A prime lens has one fixed focal length, so you zoom by walking. Which one fits depends on how much of the scene you want in each frame.

## How much wider is 35 mm than 50 mm?

On a full-frame camera, a 35 mm lens covers about 54 degrees across the long side of the frame and a 50 mm lens about 40 degrees. Those figures are the angle of view, the width of the scene that fits across the frame, measured in degrees. From the same spot, the 35 mm lens shows roughly 40 per cent more width.

| | 35 mm | 50 mm |
|---|---|---|
| Angle of view, long side (full frame) | about 54° | about 40° |
| Angle of view, diagonal (full frame) | about 63° | about 47° |
| Equivalent view on a 1.5× APS-C body | about 52 mm | about 75 mm |
| Distance for a head-and-shoulders portrait, camera held upright | about 0.5 m | about 0.7 m |
| Distance for a full-length adult, camera held upright | about 1.8 m | about 2.5 m |

Treat the distances as starting points. They assume 50 cm of subject across the tall side of the frame for the portrait and a 1.8 m adult for the full-length shot, and every lens has its own minimum focus distance.

## Does the focal length change perspective?

Focal length alone does not change it. Perspective, meaning the relative size of near and far objects, depends on where you stand. The lens only decides how much of that view you keep.

A face shot from 0.5 m shows a larger nose against the ears than the same face shot from 2 m, and the shorter distance causes that on any lens. With a 35 mm lens you stand closer to fill the frame with a person, so near features look larger and more background fits in. Step back with a 50 mm lens and the proportions look a little more even, while less of the surroundings appears.

You can test this in ten minutes. Frame a friend head to shoulders at 35 mm, then walk back until the framing matches at 50 mm, and compare the two files side by side. Look through the best frames you keep in [an online portfolio](https://anjajepsen.carrd.co/) and you will notice the same pattern in your own work: tight crops from close range look more dramatic than the same crop taken from a few steps back.

## What does the crop factor do to each lens?

The crop factor multiplies the printed focal length to give the full-frame view. On a camera with a 1.5× sensor, a 35 mm lens frames like a 52 mm lens and a 50 mm lens frames like a 75 mm lens.

The [crop factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_factor) is the ratio of the full-frame diagonal (43.3 mm) to the diagonal of your sensor. APS-C bodies are usually 1.5×, and Canon's are 1.6×. On APS-C, then, the 35 mm lens already gives a standard view and the 50 mm lens a short telephoto view, which feels tight indoors.

For the full-frame view of 35 mm on an APS-C body, look for a lens of about 22 to 24 mm. The view of 50 mm needs about 31 to 33 mm. Perspective still follows your distance, so the shorter lens changes only the framing.

## Which lens suits street and travel?

For most people, 35 mm suits both. A full-length adult fits in the frame from about 1.8 m, so you can include a person and the doorway, stall or street behind them without stepping far back.

In a crowded street, a 50 mm lens asks for about 2.5 m for the same full-length frame, and that extra distance is often where a passer-by walks into the shot. Indoors, in narrow lanes and in small cafés, 35 mm saves you from backing into a wall. A single focal length also gives a set of pictures a consistent look, which helps when you share your travel pictures on a [one-page website](https://gratis-4294591.jouwweb.nl/) and want the set to read as one body of work.

The drawback is clutter at the edges. At 35 mm a bright sign or a half-visible bystander slips into the corners more easily, so you have to watch them. A 50 mm lens trims the edges for you.

## Which lens suits portraits?

The 50 mm lens fits head-and-shoulders and half-length portraits. You stand about 0.7 m from the subject for a head-and-shoulders frame, which is comfortable for both of you, and the background is cropped down to a simple backdrop.

At [outdoor photoshoots](https://www.zupyak.com/p/3958662/t/from-bump-to-beautiful-exploring-the-delights-of-outdoor-maternity-photoshoots) you can usually step back as far as you like, so 50 mm is easy to work with. Indoors, in a small room with two or three people, 35 mm often fits the group where 50 mm cannot, and it gives an environmental portrait where the room tells part of the story.

An 85 mm lens is a common next purchase for more working distance and a softer background. Starting with 50 mm shows you quickly whether you want that.

## Does the maximum aperture differ?

Both focal lengths are commonly sold with an f/1.8 version and a faster f/1.4 version. The faster versions usually cost more and weigh more. Aperture is how wide the lens opens, written as an f-number; a smaller number lets in more light.

Many kit zooms open to f/3.5 at their wide end, so f/1.8 is about two stops brighter. Two stops means four times the light, so you can raise the shutter speed from 1/60 s to 1/250 s at the same ISO, or drop the ISO from 3200 to 800. A common rule of thumb for handheld shots is a shutter speed of at least 1 divided by the focal length: 1/40 s with 35 mm, 1/50 s with 50 mm. Image stabilisation lets you go below that.

At the same f-number, a 50 mm lens renders the background softer than a 35 mm lens when you frame the subject at the same size. That favours 50 mm when blur is your main reason for buying. In low light the two lenses are equal at the same f-number, so let the angle of view decide.

## How do you decide before buying?

Start with what you already shoot on your zoom, because your own photos are a more reliable guide than a general rule. Then try each focal length for a day before you buy.

1. Open your last few hundred photos in a catalogue and filter by focal length. On APS-C, convert with the crop factor first.
2. Most keepers near 35 mm point to the 35 mm lens, and a cluster near 50 mm points to the 50 mm.
3. Set your zoom to 35 mm and tape it there for a day. Do the same at 50 mm on another day. Note which day you enjoyed more and which pictures you kept.
4. Ask a local camera shop, which you can find through [a business directory](https://www.dialo.de/fotograf/muenchen/anja-jepsen-fotografie-y90921853), whether you can fit both lenses to your body.

When the count is close, take the 35 mm lens. Cropping a 35 mm frame to a 50 mm view keeps about half the pixels, so a 24 megapixel file gives a 12 megapixel picture, which is still enough for most uses. You cannot make a 50 mm frame wider.
