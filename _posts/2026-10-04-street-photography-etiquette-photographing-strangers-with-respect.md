Street photography means pointing a camera at people who never agreed to appear in a picture. Most of them never notice, and a few do. In that moment your behaviour matters more than the focal length in your hand, and a handful of habits covers almost every situation.

## Do you need permission to photograph strangers?

In the UK you usually do not need permission to take the picture, but you do need to think about it before you publish, sell or share it. Where a person is in a private setting, or the use is commercial, the answer changes. A legal picture can still be unwelcome, so good manners go further than the law asks.

UK law is permissive. Taking [photographs in a public place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_photography) is generally legal whether or not the people in them consent. Other countries differ. Some European countries protect a person's right to their own image and restrict publication without consent, while the United States leaves street photographers wide room. Before you travel with a camera, look up the local rules.

Three situations change the answer: spaces that are private even though they look public, anything with a commercial purpose, and people who are plainly vulnerable. Treat each as a reason to ask first or to keep the camera down.

## How do you shoot without intruding?

Work openly with a small camera at a modest focal length, set up so you can shoot quickly. Without a long lens to hide behind, anyone who looks at you can see what you are doing. Someone who meets your eye gets a smile and a nod.

| Situation | Focal length | Settings | Approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crowded pavement or market | 35 mm | f/8, 1/250 s, focus fixed at 3 m | Keep walking, keep the camera visible |
| Quiet street, one subject | 50 mm | f/5.6, 1/250 s, autofocus | Make eye contact before you raise the camera |
| Evening, low light | 35 mm | f/2, 1/125 s, ISO raised as needed | Turn off the focus-assist lamp and the shutter beep |

Zone focusing means setting the focus distance once and letting depth of field, the band of distance that looks sharp, do the rest. At 35 mm and f/8, focus set to 3 m keeps roughly 2 m to 7 m acceptably sharp on a full-frame camera. You can raise the camera and shoot without hunting for focus, which also means less time with the lens pointed at someone.

Practise first among people who expect cameras. Photo walks and courses run by local groups are a low-pressure way to start, and a [regional photography wiki](https://www.muenchenwiki.de/wiki/Fotografie) is a reasonable place to look for them.

## What should you do when someone objects?

Stop, listen and delete the frame if they ask you to. Deleting costs you one photograph, while arguing about your rights can turn a small moment into a confrontation.

1. Lower the camera and look at the person.
2. Apologise in plain words: "Sorry, I didn't mean to bother you."
3. Offer to show the picture. If they want it gone, delete it while they watch.
4. Look for automatic backups. When your camera or phone syncs to the cloud, remove that copy too.
5. Walk away from an angry person rather than explain.

A smile and a nod from the person are a reasonable yes, while silence and a turned shoulder are not. Once someone agrees after a short chat, you can take another frame and offer to send them a copy.

## When should you leave the camera down?

Leave it down for children you do not know, for people in distress and for places where people reasonably expect privacy. In each case, ask first or do not shoot.

Other people's children are the obvious case. A parent who sees a stranger photograph their child has good reason to be alarmed, even when the intent is harmless, and that reaction is the practical test whatever the law allows. To photograph children, arrange it with the parents beforehand, for instance as [photo sessions at home](https://startups.snapmunk.com/blog/mobile-newborn-photography-capturing-life-s-first-moments-in-the-comfort-of-your-home), where everyone has time to agree.

Someone crying on a bench, an argument or a person being treated by paramedics is not a subject for a picture. Put the camera away, and offer help if someone needs it. When emergency crews ask you to move back, move back.

Shopping centres, stations and event sites look public but are often privately owned, and they set their own rules for photography. Look for signs and follow what staff tell you.

## What do you check before publishing?

Ask three questions. Could the person be recognised? Could the picture embarrass them? Is it for sale, or only for sharing? If any answer worries you, get consent or choose another frame.

Captions deserve the same care as the picture. Leave out names, the street where someone lives and any label that judges the person, such as "drunk" or "homeless". Switch off GPS tagging on your camera or phone as well, because image files can carry coordinates in their metadata.

Read the submission rules before you upload anything. Many [stock photo sites](https://pxhere.com/en/photographer/4217108) ask for a signed model release when a recognisable person appears, and a street candid rarely comes with one.

If you exhibit or take commissions, give people a way to reach you and ask for a picture to be taken down. [Business directories](https://www.finderr.de/anjajepsen.de-fotograf-muenchen-anja-jepsen-fotografie-101848383) are one place to list a contact address, and a plain contact line on your own portfolio does the same job.

## How do you decide in the moment?

Imagine showing the picture to the person, face to face. If you would be happy to hand them the screen, keep going. If you would hesitate, lower the camera and walk on, because the street offers another frame within a minute.
