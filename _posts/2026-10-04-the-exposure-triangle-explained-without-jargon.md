Every photograph balances three settings: aperture, shutter speed and ISO. Change one and the picture gets brighter or darker, unless you change another to compensate. Each setting also alters something else in the image, such as sharpness, blur or grain. Once you know those side effects, you can choose settings on purpose instead of leaving the decision to the camera.

## What does the exposure triangle control?

It controls how much light reaches the sensor and how strongly the camera records it. Aperture and shutter speed set the amount of light. ISO sets how much the camera amplifies the signal. The three are linked, so a change in one needs a matching change in another to keep the same brightness.

The unit for that matching is the stop. One stop means double or half the light. The same three controls carried over from [film cameras to digital ones](https://anjajepsen.gitbook.io/evolution-of-photography), and the arithmetic has not changed.

Open the aperture by one stop and you can halve the exposure time. Raise ISO from 100 to 200 and you can do the same. In both cases the brightness stays the same and only the side effects change.

## What does aperture do?

Aperture is the opening inside the lens, written as an f-number such as f/2.8 or f/8. A small number means a wide opening: more light and a thinner slice of the scene in focus. A large number means a narrow opening: less light and more of the scene in focus.

The f-number is the focal length divided by the diameter of the opening. Each step up the [f-number scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-number), from f/2.8 to f/4 for example, halves the light. The full sequence is f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16.

The side effect is depth of field, the range of distances that look sharp. At f/1.8 and a close distance, the sharp zone can be thinner than a face, so one eye is crisp and the other is not. At f/11 most of a scene looks sharp from front to back. From about f/16 onwards on many lenses, fine detail starts to soften because light bends around the edges of the opening, an effect called diffraction. Some lenses are also slightly soft when fully open.

A faster lens, meaning one with a wider maximum aperture, costs more than a slower one. Some camera shops let you mount a lens on your own body, so check [opening hours in a directory](https://finde-offen.de/muenchen/anja-jepsen-fotografie-5015260) and take your camera along.

## What does shutter speed do?

Shutter speed is how long the sensor collects light, written as fractions of a second such as 1/250 s. Fast speeds freeze movement. Slow speeds let it blur. Held in your hands, the camera also records your own shake at slow speeds.

The usual sequence is 1/30, 1/60, 1/125, 1/250, 1/500 and 1/1000 s, and each step halves the light. A handheld starting rule is a speed no slower than one over the focal length: 1/50 s on a 50 mm lens, 1/200 s on a 200 mm lens. Lens or body stabilisation lets you go slower. A person walking across the frame needs roughly 1/250 s to look sharp. Faster action needs 1/1000 s or more.

A fast speed costs light. At 1/1000 s the sensor sees very little, so the aperture must open or ISO must rise.

## What does ISO do?

ISO sets how much the camera amplifies the light it has recorded. Low values such as ISO 100 give a clean image. High values such as ISO 3200 brighten a dark scene but add noise, which looks like speckled grain. Doubling the ISO value brightens the image by one stop.

High ISO adds no light to the scene: the camera amplifies signal and noise together, and noise shows first in the shadows. The lowest native setting, called base ISO, is often 100 or 200, depending on the camera. How high you can go before the noise bothers you depends on the sensor. Test your own camera by shooting one scene at ISO 400, 1600 and 6400, then view each frame at full size.

## How do the three settings trade off?

Any setting that adds light also costs you something. Choose the cost the scene in front of you can tolerate.

| Setting | Move for more light | What you gain | What you pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aperture | f/8 to f/5.6 | Blurrier background, faster speeds | Thinner zone of focus |
| Shutter speed | 1/250 s to 1/125 s | Lower ISO or smaller aperture | Motion blur, camera shake |
| ISO | 400 to 800 | Usable speeds in dim light | More noise |

## What do the settings look like in real situations?

Decide which effect has priority, whether that is background blur, frozen movement or low noise. Fix that setting first and let the other two follow. The starting point for daylight is the "sunny 16" rule: f/16, a shutter speed of one over the ISO, so 1/100 s at ISO 100 (the nearest real setting is 1/125 s).

### Portrait in bright sun

You want a soft background, so aperture comes first: f/2.8. Going from f/16 to f/2.8 is five stops more light, so the shutter must be five stops faster. That gives 1/3200 s at ISO 100. The camera must support that speed, so check its limit before you plan around it.

### Cyclist on an overcast day

Here the priority is movement, so set 1/800 s. An overcast sky is about f/8 at 1/100 s and ISO 100. Moving to 1/800 s costs three stops. Open to f/5.6 for one stop and raise ISO to 400 for two. The result is f/5.6, 1/800 s, ISO 400.

### Friend reading in a lit room

The subject is still, so a slow speed is safe. With a 50 mm lens, 1/60 s is enough to avoid shake. Evening room light is many stops dimmer than overcast daylight, so f/1.8 and ISO 400 is a sensible first try. Check the preview and raise ISO to 800 if the frame looks dark.

## Which mistakes do beginners make most often?

Most come from fixing one setting and forgetting the other two. These five turn up again and again:

- Leaving ISO high after an evening shoot. The next morning the camera records bright daylight at ISO 3200 and the noise has no reason to be there. Check ISO when you switch the camera on.
- Shooting wide open for a group. At f/1.8 only one row stays sharp. Use f/5.6 or f/8 when people stand at different distances.
- Choosing a handheld speed that is too slow. If frames look soft although the focus point is right, shake is the usual cause.
- Stopping down to f/22 for maximum sharpness. Diffraction softens the image and the shutter must stay open longer.
- Judging exposure on the small rear screen. It hides noise and slight focus errors, so zoom in on a few frames.

Notes help you spot your own habits. Write down aperture, speed and ISO for the frames you like, in a notes app or on [a blogging platform](https://teletype.in/@anjajepsen), and compare them a week later.

## Which setting should you change first?

Start with the effect the subject demands. Movement sets the shutter speed, depth of field sets the aperture and ISO fills the remaining gap. Work in that order:

1. Choose the shutter speed that stops the movement you care about.
2. Choose the aperture that gives the depth you want.
3. Raise ISO until the exposure looks right.
4. If the noise is too strong, accept a slower speed or a wider aperture and shoot again.

Next time you go out, take ten frames of one subject and change a single setting between them, compensating with another. Comparing the results on a large screen shows the side effects more clearly than a chart can. Following a few [photography blogs](https://rss.feedspot.com/germany_photography_rss_feeds/) gives you plenty of other subjects to practise on.
