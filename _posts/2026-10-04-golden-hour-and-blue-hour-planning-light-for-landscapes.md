Good evening light lasts a few minutes. Work out the sun's position, the weather and your exposure before you leave the car, so that you can spend those minutes shooting.

## What are golden hour and blue hour?

Golden hour is the period after sunrise or before sunset when the sun is low and the light is [redder and softer than at midday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_hour_%28photography%29). Blue hour is the twilight just before sunrise or after sunset, when the sun is below the horizon and the sky turns deep blue. Neither lasts exactly an hour, and the length changes with season and latitude.

The two phases suit different subjects. Golden hour gives you directional light that models hills, trees and rock faces with long shadows. Blue hour gives you an even, cool sky and, if there are lights in the scene, a pleasing contrast between warm and cold colour.

| Phase | Sun position | Light | Starting settings (tripod, ISO 100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Golden hour | Low, above the horizon | Warm, directional, long shadows | f/8 to f/11, about 1/15 s |
| Sunset or sunrise | At the horizon | Very high contrast | Bracket three frames |
| Blue hour | Just below the horizon, during twilight | Soft, cool, even | f/8, about 2 s to 8 s |

The shutter speeds are examples for a scene with a bright sky. Check the histogram on the back of the camera and adjust from there.

## How do you find where the sun will be?

Check the sun's rising or setting azimuth (its compass bearing) and the exact times for your date and location. Sun-position apps, printed ephemeris tables and the sunrise and sunset tables in many diaries all give you this. An app that draws the sun's path over a map also shows whether a ridge or a tree line will block the light early.

Pictures other photographers have posted from the same place, on photo sites or [creative networks](https://dasauge.co.uk/-anjajepsen/), help you judge which direction suits it.

1. Look up the sunrise or sunset time and the bearing of the sun for the day you plan to shoot.
2. Place the sun on a map or a satellite view and check what stands between it and your subject.
3. Decide whether you want the light behind you, to the side or against the lens. Side light shows texture. Backlight gives silhouettes and glow, but it needs bracketing.

Arrive 45 minutes before the time you want. The light changes quickly once the sun nears the horizon, and you need time to set up a composition while the light is still ordinary. Check access as well: gate times, parking and whether the walk is safe in the dark. [Local business directories](https://www.branchenorte.de/Anja-Jepsen-Fotografie-39551713.html) often list opening hours for the car parks and cafés nearby.

## Which weather suits evening light?

Broken cloud works well, because it catches colour while the sun still reaches the scene. A clear sky gives clean warm light but a plain sky. A solid grey layer gives no colour at all.

Check the forecast for cloud height as well as cloud cover. High, thin cloud lights up from below after sunset, while a thick low bank on the horizon blocks the sun before the colour begins. For sunset, look at the western horizon, and for sunrise the eastern one. If there is a clear gap there, the low sun can light the clouds above you. Rain clearing from the sun's side often produces strong colour, and mist in valleys adds layers. The forecast only narrows the choice, so make the final decision on site.

## What white balance should you use?

Shoot RAW and set the white balance on purpose. Auto white balance tries to neutralise the colour of the light, which removes the warmth you came for. Daylight, at about 5,500 K, keeps golden hour warm. For a stronger glow, try Cloudy or a setting near 6,500 K.

Most beginners expect the Kelvin setting to work the other way round. A lower value tells the camera the light is warm and corrects by adding blue, so the picture cools. A higher value does the opposite. For blue hour, try 4,000 K to 5,000 K and judge the sky by eye. Too low a value gives a heavy, artificial blue, and Tungsten at 3,200 K usually overshoots.

Because RAW files keep the setting open, you can refine the colour later. Choose one setting for the whole series, so that every frame from the evening matches.

## How do you bracket exposures at low light?

Bracketing means taking several frames at different exposures and combining them. At sunrise and sunset the sky is much brighter than the ground, and one frame cannot hold both. A bracket of three frames, 2 EV apart (EV is an exposure step, one doubling of light), usually covers it.

1. Put the camera on a tripod and switch to aperture priority at f/8 to f/11, ISO 100.
2. Turn on autobracketing for three frames at 2 EV apart, with a two-second timer or a remote release.
3. Change only the shutter speed between frames. A fixed aperture keeps the depth of field the same in every frame.
4. Check the darkest frame for detail in the sun's glow and the brightest for detail in the shadows.
5. Merge the frames with HDR merge in your editing software, or blend them by hand with layer masks.

If the scene moves, such as waves or grass, a graduated filter, which darkens the sky only, can replace the bracket.

## How do you build a foreground?

Choose the foreground before the light arrives. A rock, a fence line, a stream or a curve of path gives the eye a way into the frame, and low sun lights its texture. Walk the spot in daylight so you know where to stand when the colour appears.

Use a wide lens, 16 mm to 24 mm on a full-frame camera, and move close to the foreground. Focus about one third of the way into the scene at f/11 as a starting point. If the nearest object is within a metre of the lens, take a second frame focused on it and blend the two. Check the corners at 100 per cent magnification before the light fades.

Keep notes so the planning improves each time: date, sun bearing, weather, settings and what you would change. A notes app, paper or [a simple one-page site](http://www.geocities.ws/anjajepsen/) all work as a scouting log.

## What should you do first?

Pick one place you can reach in under 30 minutes. Check the sun's bearing for the next clear evening, scout it once in daylight, then return 45 minutes before sunset with a tripod and a three-frame bracket. After the first evening, compare your frames with your notes and change one thing, either the position, the white balance or the foreground.

Kept for a year, a diary on [a blogging platform](https://profile.hatena.ne.jp/anjajepsen/) lets you compare the same spot across seasons and see which conditions were worth the trip.
