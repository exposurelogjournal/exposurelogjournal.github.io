Handheld night shots usually come out soft or noisy because the camera has too little light and too little time. A long exposure, meaning a shutter time of a second or more, fixes both problems. It needs a steady camera, accurate focus and a few starting settings that you adjust one step at a time.

## What kit do you need?

You need a sturdy tripod, a way to fire the shutter without touching the camera, and a torch. Everything else is optional.

Choose a tripod that stays put in a breeze. Extend the thick upper leg sections first, keep the centre column low and hang your bag from the hook if there is one. For the trigger, use a cable release or wireless remote. Without one, the two-second self-timer lets the shake from your finger die away before the shutter opens.

Switch off image stabilisation while the camera sits on a tripod, because the stabiliser can add small movements of its own. On a DSLR, turn on mirror lock-up or an exposure delay mode, since the mirror can shake the body at shutter times around a second. A headtorch with a red mode lets you read dials without ruining your night vision.

## How do you focus in the dark?

Switch to manual focus, magnify the live view on a distant bright light and turn the focus ring until the point of light is as small as it gets. Autofocus hunts when contrast is low, so it often misses at night.

1. Set the lens to MF and press the magnify button until the live view shows a 10x crop.
2. Aim at a streetlamp, a lit window or a distant sign.
3. Turn the ring slowly until the point of light starts to swell again, then turn back.
4. Take one test frame and zoom in on the review to confirm.

Do not trust the infinity mark on the lens barrel. Many lenses focus slightly beyond infinity, so the end stop is often soft. Once focus is set, leave the ring alone, and check again if you change the focal length on a zoom.

## Which settings should you start with?

Use manual mode with f/8, ISO 100 and a shutter time of 10 to 30 seconds, then correct from the first frame. Most lenses resolve more detail around f/5.6 to f/8 than at wider or narrower apertures. ISO 100 records less noise than any higher setting, so the long shutter time has to supply the light.

| Scene | Aperture | ISO | Shutter time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Floodlit building or bridge | f/8 | 100 | 10 to 20 s |
| Traffic light trails | f/11 | 100 | 15 to 30 s |
| Street lit only by lamps | f/5.6 | 400 | 15 to 30 s |

Most cameras stop their timed exposures at 30 seconds. For anything longer, use Bulb mode, where the shutter stays open for as long as you hold the release. A remote with a lock saves you from holding the button down for a minute or more.

White balance needs setting by hand, because automatic modes shift from frame to frame under mixed lamps. Start near 4000 K and adjust until the lamps look the colour you remember. If you shoot raw, you can change it later without loss.

## How do you check the exposure?

Read the histogram and the highlight warning after the first frame instead of judging by screen brightness. A rear screen looks brighter in the dark than it is.

Change one thing at a time. Doubling the shutter time adds one stop of light, and halving it removes one. If large areas of the frame flash as clipped highlights, shorten the exposure or close the aperture by a stop. Small clipped lamps are normal, since a bare bulb is far brighter than anything else in the scene.

To compose faster, raise the ISO to 1600 and open the aperture to f/4. A 16 second exposure at f/8 and ISO 100 then becomes about a quarter of a second. Frame the shot with those quick tests, then return to your low-noise settings for the real frame. Keep a short log of aperture, shutter time and ISO for each keeper, in a notebook or on [a blogging site](https://click4r.com/en/blogs/anja-jepsen-fotografie-14395824), so you can reuse what worked.

## How do you capture light trails?

Fix the camera beside a road and use 15 to 30 seconds at f/11 and ISO 100. Press the shutter when traffic is flowing and let several vehicles pass.

Vehicle lights draw bright streaks in [long-exposure photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-exposure_photography), while the road and buildings stay sharp. Headlights record as white and tail lights as red. A narrow aperture such as f/11 keeps the streaks thin and stops strong lights from washing out. A higher viewpoint, such as a footbridge, shows the curve of the road and gives longer, cleaner lines.

Short exposures give dotted trails, because the gaps between vehicles show. A 20 second frame on a busy road usually joins them up. Expect to shoot many frames, as the pattern of traffic decides the result and you cannot control it.

## How do you keep noise down?

Keep ISO low, expose for longer rather than raising ISO, and shoot raw. Noise is the grain that appears when the sensor receives too little light and the signal gets amplified.

Place the exposure toward the right of the histogram without clipping the lamps. Underexposed frames lifted in editing show far more noise than frames exposed properly in the camera. Frames that look clean on [a one-page site](https://anjajepsen.mystrikingly.com/) at small size can still show grain at 100 per cent, so judge them at full size.

Long exposure noise reduction makes the camera take a second, dark frame of the same length and subtract it, which removes hot pixels. A 30 second shot then takes 60 seconds to finish. Leave it off while you experiment and turn it on for final frames if bright speckles appear. The sensor also warms up during a long session, which raises noise, so pause now and then.

## How do you stay safe at night?

Stay on the pavement or a bridge, never on the carriageway, and keep your kit out of the way of other people. Tell someone where you are going.

Wear something reflective when you shoot near traffic, and keep the tripod legs tucked in so nobody trips over them. Check your footing before you step back from the camera, because you will be looking at the screen and not the ground. Stay clear of unprotected edges, and never lean on a railing to steady the shot.

Car parks, forecourts and private estates often look ideal, but ask permission first. Use [a company directory](https://unternehmensauskunft.com/anja-jepsen-fotografie-36758639.html) to find who runs the site if there is no sign. A short, polite message usually settles it.

## Where should you start tonight?

Pick one lit, still subject close to home, such as a floodlit building. Set f/8, ISO 100 and 15 seconds, take a frame and adjust by one stop.

When that frame is sharp and well exposed, move to a road and try light trails. Afterwards, choose a few keepers and share them on [free photo sites](https://skitterphoto.com/photographers/81758) to see how they hold up at web size.
